Why structured reservoirs remember

August 1, 2026 · 8 min read

The spectral structure of an environment determines whether an emitter forgets its past quickly or retains a measurable memory of it.

A useful starting point is a two-level emitter coupled to a continuum of bosonic modes. In the rotating-wave approximation, the Hamiltonian can be written as

\[ \hat H = \frac{\omega_q}{2}\hat\sigma_z + \int dk\,\omega_k \hat a_k^\dagger \hat a_k + \int dk\, \left( g_k \hat\sigma_+ \hat a_k + g_k^* \hat a_k^\dagger \hat\sigma_- \right). \tag{1} \]

The environment enters the reduced dynamics through the dispersion relation \(\omega_k\) and the couplings \(g_k\). These quantities are commonly summarized by the spectral density

\[ J(\omega) = 2\pi\int dk\,|g_k|^2\, \delta\!\left(\omega-\omega_k\right). \tag{2} \]

From a spectrum to a memory kernel

In the single-excitation sector, eliminating the field amplitudes produces an integro-differential equation for the excited-state amplitude \(c_e(t)\):

\[ \dot c_e(t) = -\int_0^t d\tau\, K(t-\tau)c_e(\tau), \qquad K(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int d\omega\,J(\omega) e^{-i(\omega-\omega_q)t}. \tag{3} \]

The kernel \(K(t)\) measures how strongly the present evolution depends on earlier values of the amplitude. A broad and nearly flat spectrum generally gives a sharply localized kernel. A band edge, a narrow resonance, a time delay, or another strong spectral feature can instead produce a kernel with a long temporal tail.

What changes physically?

Once the environment has a significant memory time, the dynamics need not be a simple exponential. Depending on the system, one may encounter oscillatory decay, partial population trapping, delayed revivals, or long-lived emitter–field bound states.

The environment is not only a sink for excitations. Its geometry and spectrum can become active ingredients in the quantum dynamics.

This point is especially useful in waveguide QED, where dispersion, propagation distance, and the spatial structure of an emitter can all be engineered. In such systems, non-Markovian behavior is often easier to understand by looking directly at the spectrum and the relevant propagation times.

A practical diagnostic

Before applying a Markov approximation, it is useful to compare the characteristic variation scale of \(J(\omega)\) with the linewidth expected for the emitter. One should also compare propagation delays with the emitter lifetime. When these scales are not well separated, retaining the full frequency dependence or the explicit time delay is usually the safer description.
